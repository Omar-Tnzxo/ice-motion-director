# Short form: first three seconds, open loops and entertainment review

Merged from nateherkai/herk-edit-kit (MIT, Use Permission: short-form-edit, curiosity-and-entertainment, editing playbook), majedphotos/video-ad-editor (MIT: written hooks), Omar-Tnzxo/video-production-studio, digitalsamba/claude-code-video-toolkit (MIT), AbubakrChan/product-launch-motion (MIT). Hook copy research and the pitch round belong to the ice-direction skill; dialect checks of written hooks to the ice-arabic skill.

## 1. The priority gate

The priority test (is someone convinced in the first 3 seconds to watch to the end), the hook rules and the rule that a raw recording caps the edit are owned by the `ice-ideas` skill, `references/hooks-and-openers.md` sections 1 and 2. Apply them before effects, asset volume or polish.

## 2. First three seconds as a separate edit

Write three provisional openings in the hook sheet `ice/plan/hooks.md` exactly as the `ice-ideas` skill, `references/hooks-and-openers.md` section 5, describes, cut from the same truthful source speech so each leads into the same narration. Edit-only steps:

1. Cut the first three seconds of each opening cheaply from the real footage and compare them; never pick the loudest effect. Record the choice and why.
2. Make the first frame meaningful with what the footage holds; no mandatory stamp, flash or stop word on every video.
3. Opening sound: remove recording lead-in, align the effect's audible impact with the visual landing within two frames, shape its level and tail around the first words, dip the music briefly if helpful. Loudness is never the only hook. Check that the first spoken word survives the mix.
4. Inspect the encoded opening frame by frame as its own gate.

## 3. Story shape (house reel, illustration of one proven order)

1. Hook in the first 3 seconds: a claim or command that names the tool or person.
2. One line of credibility or context.
3. If there is a list, show all items early, blurred, as an open loop.
4. Each item: a section card, a one-line definition, two or three literal visuals, a punch-in on the key line.
5. A resolution that calls back to the hook visual.
6. Call to action: show the resource first, then a punch-in on the comment keyword.

Write the beat plan in `ice/plan/storyboard.md` before building: hook, open loop, escalation, evidence, payoff, call to action; give every shot a reason to exist. Plan the story (hook, proof, items, payoff, call to action) before any visuals.

## 4. Open loops

Open-loop rules and the ledger `ice/plan/open-loops.json` are owned by the `ice-ideas` skill: rules in `references/hooks-and-openers.md` section 6, schema in `references/templates.md` section 4. In an edit, every loop state must exist in the footage or in a graphic built from it; do not invent a product result to make a more satisfying payoff.

## 5. Rhythm and attention inside the short

- For each sequence record: expectation, development, payoff, attention (which single thing the eye follows), rhythm, sound (which cue anticipates, contacts or releases; which cuts stay quiet).
- Vary rhythm by sequence: a short cluster of discoveries, a brief anticipation, then a payoff. The 1 to 2 s event guideline is a diagnostic, not a metronome or permission to add filler. Keep longer holds when a developing action or a performance earns them, and record why. Let the speaker's expression carry a conviction beat.
- Assign transitions by editorial purpose, never by rotating through a list.
- Attention study: wide context, isolated detail, interaction, visible consequence, then back to the speaker. A new heading or caption alone is not the answer.
- Give every extended graphic beat an action with an outcome (something seats, connects, opens, assembles). Avoid settle-and-freeze title cards and identical idle bobbing on every object.
- Review adjacent shots as silhouettes with headings hidden; if two read as the same reveal, redesign the weaker one. Spend the strongest action on the most important spoken idea.
- Middle-grind scenes (roughly scenes 3 to 5 of a 15 to 20 s short): lean on visuals that read as information or evidence; type plus icon alone reads as slides.
- Audit duplication: narration and captions carry the exact words; graphics should show the example, relation, evidence or consequence. Remove a third restatement as a headline unless deliberate.
- Concept shorts with narration: scene 01 must earn the next 5 s (question or tension in the first sentence, no throat-clearing); alternate cards and motion for a pattern interrupt about every 15 s.

## 6. Written hooks over clips cut from longer talks

- Mandatory for every reel cut from an episode or interview: readable from frame 1, on screen 1.5 to 3 s (about 0.9 s plus 0.28 s per word), at least 68 px.
- 3 to 8 words, at most two lines, no final period, no emoji; built from the strongest line, never simply the first sentence said; only words the speaker said or that the clip supports. Any number must appear in the clip's speech, and the promise must resolve inside the clip.
- Use at least one hook device: a number, a contradiction, a question, curiosity, a command, a story.
- Hide all other text while the hook shows. Write three hook options for the first reel; once one is picked (the user's review card in step mode, a critic in auto mode), keep that style.

## 7. Animatic, lock, entertainment review

1. Render a low-resolution moving animatic with speech and rough sound before polishing materials. Fix predictability, missing context and weak payoffs here.
2. Lock the cut after the animatic, before polished graphics.
3. Run a separate, uninterrupted entertainment review of the animatic and of the final. Record attention-drop timestamps, predictable sequences, unresolved promises and reading overload in `ice/review/ENTERTAINMENT-REVIEW.md`. Never manufacture retention scores; never call internal hook comparisons an audience test.
4. With stills only: inspect chronological samples every 0.25 to 0.5 s plus contiguous action strips, audit every loop state, and do not call automated playback a viewing pass.
